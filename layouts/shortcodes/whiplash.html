Methods

A cycle is warm-cold-warm or cold-warm-cold, with the threshold met on both temperature changes. Intensity is the smaller of the two absolute changes: 71 → 27 → 76°F changes by 44°F, then 49°F, so its intensity is 44°F. This ensures both changes are substantial. Overlapping candidates are consolidated, strongest first.

Full-cycle span is the elapsed time from the first temperature to the final temperature, including both changes. Four days cooling followed by five days warming spans nine days. The median summarizes all detected cycles in the record under the current settings.

R² is the fraction of variation between annual values explained by the fitted curve. Linear trends are solid when their 95% change interval excludes zero, dashed otherwise.

The default is a 27°F (15°C) minimum change in each direction.

How return periods are calculated

For each eligible year, take the strongest detected reversal cycle, assigned to the year of its turning point. For a chosen intensity, count years containing at least one cycle that meets or exceeds it. Divide that count by the total eligible years to get the observed annual exceedance fraction p; the empirical annual return period is 1/p. Years with no qualifying cycle remain in the denominator. This measures recurrence of years with a cycle, rather than the average spacing between individual cycles. See the NWS explanation of annual exceedance and recurrence for that distinction.

The intensity threshold and maximum days for each change update this panel. It uses the same full-record years with at least 95% valid observations as the trend fits, including years with zero cycles. Cycles in excluded years remain visible in the timeline but do not enter these estimates; for example, the 2019 and 2026 peaks are excluded. Missing observations can still hide a year's strongest cycle. No probability distribution is fitted and no return period is extrapolated beyond observed intensities. With zero exceeding years, a finite return period cannot be estimated.

The comparison above uses the first and last 30-calendar-year windows anchored to the first and last eligible years. Each point in the history chart repeats that calculation for the preceding 30 calendar years, ending in the plotted year. At least 20 of those years must meet the 95% daily-data coverage rule; other windows are gaps. Counts are divided by the number of eligible years in that window, not by 30 automatically. The time brush does not change these windows. The comparison has its own intensity selector and shares the maximum-days setting above; the folded full-record curve follows the timeline's intensity threshold.

The table's approximate 95% intervals use the Wilson binomial method. They assume independent years with a common probability within each window, and do not account for serial dependence, station changes or missing extremes. Overlapping rolling windows share observations and are not independent samples. These intervals are descriptive guides, rather than a formal test of a trend or a change between eras.

A 10-year annual return period means a historical exceedance fraction of 10%, not a regular ten-year schedule. Treating that fraction as a probability for a future year would assume stable conditions; this record's changing frequency makes that assumption questionable. The curve is descriptive and has no confidence band.
